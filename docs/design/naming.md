## 1. Naming criteria

Derived from brand personality ("the experienced coach who gets it"), the voice guardrails (athletic not gamey, premium not cutesy, empowering not patronizing), and the strategy docs (father-son wedge in marketing, parent-child-generic product; teen drift is a core failure mode the product exists to solve).

### Guardrails (pass/fail — applied before scoring)

- **G1 — No Fit/Fitness + generic suffix.** Nothing in the FitFam/HouseFit/FamilyFitness pattern. Instantly generic, instantly forgettable.
- **G2 — No game vocabulary.** No XP, quest, level, arena, power-up phonemes. Also excludes names whose dominant association is a video game (e.g., Kratos).

### Scored criteria (0–2 each, max 20)

| # | Criterion | What "2" means |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | **Athletic register** | Sounds like training culture, not a family-activity or kids' product. A serious coach could say it with a straight face. |
| C2 | **The 14-year-old test** | A teen can say it to friends with zero embarrassment — ideally it sounds like a brand he'd wear on a shirt. Weighted as the tiebreaker: teen drift is failure mode #3. |
| C3 | **Parent-child generic** | No dad/son/father hard-coded; works verbatim for mom + daughter, blended families, adoptive families. The wedge lives in marketing, never in the name. |
| C4 | **Identity & usage potential** | Becomes household language: "we're a ___ family," "___ tonight?" Supports the north star — the streak becoming the family's identity. |
| C5 | **Speakable / spellable** | Passes the radio test: hear it once, spell it right. ≤3 syllables, works as an app-icon label. |
| C6 | **Searchable / ownable** | Findable in App Store and Google; not drowned by a common dictionary meaning or an existing giant. |
| C7 | **Domain / TM plausibility** *(UNVERIFIED)* | .com or credible variant (.app, get-, join-) plausibly acquirable; no known direct collision in fitness/sports from model knowledge. |
| C8 | **Coach-voice fit** | Sounds right in the coach's mouth and alongside the existing product vocabulary (Pact, Trials, Challenges, tiers). No collision that breaks copy. |
| C9 | **Room to grow** | Survives the Phase 3 weights arc, family-vs-family leagues, teen companion mode. Doesn't hard-code bodyweight, living room, or even fitness narrowly. |
| C10 | **No unfortunate readings** | No slang misfires, dark collocations, kid-scout associations, or cultural double meanings that create friction — including in the church-community wedge channel. |

## 2. Candidate longlist — 29 names across 6 strategies

### Strategy A — Elevate an in-product word
The product already has a vocabulary families will live inside (Pact, Trials, Challenges). Naming the brand after the mechanic makes the retention loop and the brand the same word.

1. **Pact** — the weekly consistency mechanic, elevated. "Keep the Pact" is simultaneously brand tagline, push notification, and retention loop.
2. **IronPact** — pact + iron (training-culture shorthand for serious strength). Distinctive compound.
3. **Trials** — the monthly benchmark battery. Included for completeness; collides with its own mechanic ("open Trials to do your Trials") and scores accordingly.

### Strategy B — Family-team language
The family as a roster, a side, a house.

4. **HomeTeam** — your family is the home team. Sports-native, relationship-first, and pre-built for Phase 2–3 family-vs-family leagues.
5. **Lineup** — "who's training tonight?" is literally the app's opening question. Starting-lineup energy; roster-adaptive sessions made brandable.
6. **Roster** — same insight, drier and more administrative.
7. **The Pride** — a pride of lions: the animal kingdom's family unit of apex athletes. Conceptually the purest "family of athletes" metaphor.
8. **Stronghouse** — "House" as family lineage (house mottos, House Stark energy — a register teens find cool) + strong.

### Strategy C — Borrowed from training culture
Words athletes already use for the place or ritual of work.

9. **The Yard** — calisthenics culture's word for where bodyweight work gets done; also just your backyard. "Yard time tonight."
10. **The Den** — where the family gathers (the den at home) and where the pack raises its young.
11. **Camp** — training camp, fight camp: the team that prepares you.
12. **Chalk** — "chalk up" = get ready to work.
13. **Proving Ground** — where you find out what you're made of; pairs with sacred benchmarks (Trials).
14. **Crucible** — the severe shared test that bonds and transforms (the Marines' final test is The Crucible). Shared-struggle-bonds, literally.
15. **Dugout** — where the team sits together between innings.

### Strategy D — Evocative / metaphor
16. **Forge** — you forge strength and you forge bonds; the double meaning *is* the brand.
17. **Foundry** — where strong things are made, at home.
18. **Anvil** — the stable base that takes the blows.
19. **Torch** — the Olympic torch and *passing the torch*: parent-to-child transmission without hard-coding dad/son. Marketing gold for the wedge.
20. **Stronghold** — the family as fortress.
21. **Bedrock** — the family as foundation.
22. **Groundwork** — "putting in the groundwork," at home, before anyone's watching.
23. **Cadence** — training cadence + the family's weekly rhythm; the workout bends to your life.
24. **Bloodline** — athletic lineage; "bloodlines" is real sports commentary language.

### Strategy E — Coined
Honest finding: coined names fit this brand poorly. The "experienced coach" voice is plain-spoken Anglo-Saxon; invented words read startup-y against it. Two included to show the ceiling of the strategy:

25. **Strongkind** — "the strong kind (of family)"; *kind* and *kin* share a root.
26. **Ironkin** — iron + kin.

### Strategy F — Compound (family × strength × home)
27. **Strongside** — football's strong side of the formation + "stronger side by side." Your family is your strong side. (Bonus association: *Remember the Titans* — "Strong side! Left side!" — an iconic brotherhood moment.)
28. **Homefield** — home-field advantage. Training at home isn't the budget option; it's your edge. Directly weaponizes the no-gym positioning.
29. **Homestrong** — a strong home (Livestrong/Army Strong construction).

### Killed before scoring (known collisions or guardrail failures — all REPORTED, UNVERIFIED)

| Name | Why killed |
|---|---|
| Huddle | Conceptually near-perfect (family gathers, coach calls the session) — but Hudl is a giant sports-video platform and Slack owns "huddles" in daily speech. |
| Ladder | Rep ladders are real bodyweight programming — but Ladder is an established strength-training app. Direct category collision. |
| Tempo | Tempo Studio, existing home-fitness hardware brand. |
| Kickoff | Existing fitness-coaching app of that name. |
| Rally | Rally Health, major health brand. |
| Powerhouse | Powerhouse Gym, national chain. |
| Basecamp / Homebase | 37signals / existing scheduling app + UK retail chain. |
| Varsity | Teen-cool but excludes the parent, reads school-age-only; Varsity Brands owns the space. |
| The Rack | Training-culture pedigree, but the slang reading fails a family brand outright. |
| Kratos | Greek "strength" — but he's a video-game protagonist. Fails G2. |
| FitFam / HouseFit / any Fit-compound | Fails G1. |

## 4. Top 5

### 1. Strongside — 19/20

**Rationale.** The most premium-athletic sound on the board. "Strong side" is real football language (the strong side of the formation), and the brand reading — *your family is your strong side; you're stronger side by side* — lands in one beat without hard-coding any family shape. It's the name that best survives the brand's hardest constraint: athletic enough for a serious coach, warm enough for a family, cool enough for a teen. The *Remember the Titans* echo ("Strong side! Left side!") is a brotherhood-through-training reference, which is exactly the emotional territory.

**The 14-year-old test.** A friend sees the app or the shirt: "Strongside — it's a training app." Sounds like an athletic brand, requires zero family disclosure, zero cringe. Best-in-class; this is the name a teen would voluntarily wear.

**Tagline pairing.** *"Strongside. Stronger side by side."* Alt: *"Family is your strong side."* Legacy register also works: *"Strongside — Train Together. Build Champions."*

**Risks.** (1) Small gyms/CrossFit affiliates plausibly already use "Strong Side" — common-law trademark risk in class 41 (REPORTED, UNVERIFIED). (2) strongside.com almost certainly taken (UNVERIFIED); .app/get- variants needed. (3) The metaphor needs the tagline to fully land on first contact — it's not self-explanating the way HomeTeam is. (4) Heard aloud, could be transcribed as two words.

---

### 2. HomeTeam — 18/20

**Rationale.** The most mission-aligned concept in the sprint: the product's whole thesis is that the family is a team, and "home team" is the sports phrase that already means exactly that. It's relationship-first (names the people, not the workout), it pre-builds the Phase 2–3 league layer (home team vs. other families' teams — the language is already there), and it makes the north star literal: the streak becomes the family's identity because the family has a team name. "Home" also quietly carries the no-gym positioning.

**The 14-year-old test.** "Me and my dad train on HomeTeam." Sporty enough to pass clean; the word "home" makes family explicit, which costs a little teen-cool versus Strongside, but the sports register covers it. No cringe, mild wholesomeness.

**Tagline pairing.** *"HomeTeam. The first team you ever make."* (Double meaning: form the team / make the cut.) The legacy line also pairs perfectly: *"HomeTeam — Train Together. Build Champions."*

**Risks.** (1) Existing marks across classes — a home-inspection franchise and at least one past healthcare startup have used HomeTeam (REPORTED, UNVERIFIED); class 41/9 clearance is the make-or-break check. (2) "Home team" is a common phrase — App Store and Google search will be noisy; ownability depends on the exact-match name plus a modifier. (3) Reads slightly rec-league-wholesome to a hardcore-athletic ear; the coach voice and visual identity must carry the seriousness.

---

### 3. Homefield — 18/20

**Rationale.** Same family-and-home insight as HomeTeam, expressed as *place and advantage* instead of people: home-field advantage. It directly converts the product's biggest perceived weakness ("it's just home workouts") into the brand claim — training at home with your family isn't the budget option, it's the edge no gym can give you. Sounds like a college-athletics brand, which is a register teens actively like.

**The 14-year-old test.** "It's called Homefield." Reads as sports apparel/athletics; no family disclosure needed; passes clean.

**Tagline pairing.** *"Homefield. Home is your advantage."* Alt: *"Every living room is a training ground."*

**Risks.** (1) Homefield Apparel is a known college-sports apparel brand — adjacent space, sports context, real confusion risk (REPORTED, UNVERIFIED; this is the first thing to check). (2) Names the place, not the relationship — the brand's core is the people; marketing has to supply the warmth. (3) homefield.com presumed gone (UNVERIFIED).

---

### 4. Pact — 16/20

**Rationale.** The strategic wildcard: elevate the in-product mechanic to the brand. The Weekly Pact is already the product's retention loop and its moral center ("consistency over perfection; showing up is the win"). Naming the app Pact unifies brand, mechanic, tagline, and push notification into one word: *Keep the Pact.* It's also the least "fitness app" name on the list — it names the commitment, not the workout — which means it survives every future pivot (weights, leagues, teen mode) untouched. One syllable, spellable, and a teen can invoke it without ever sounding like he's describing an app.

**The 14-year-old test.** "We've got a pact — three sessions a week." He's describing a commitment between people, not a product. Passes almost invisibly; arguably the lowest-cringe answer possible.

**Tagline pairing.** *"Pact. Keep it."* / *"Keep the Pact."* Structural bonus: the tagline is the retention notification.

**Risks.** (1) Prior art: a fitness-accountability app named Pact (formerly GymPact) operated until ~2017 — dead, but the trademark history in fitness classes needs checking; PACT is also an existing apparel brand, and the PACT Act (US veterans law) currently dominates the term in search (all REPORTED, UNVERIFIED). (2) Dark collocations exist ("suicide pact"; a well-known Picoult novel of that title) — low salience but real. (3) Least athletic name in the top 5; the coach voice must supply all the sweat. (4) Brand/mechanic double duty needs care in copy — keep the mechanic "the Weekly Pact" and the brand "Pact."

---

### 5. The Yard — 17/20

**Rationale.** The best borrowed-from-training-culture candidate. In calisthenics culture "the yard" already means the place where bodyweight work gets done — hard, minimal, no machines — which is precisely this product's training DNA (pull-up bar, bodyweight, wherever you are). And it doubles as the literal backyard/living room where the family trains. "Yard time tonight" is native usage language nobody has to be taught.

**The 14-year-old test.** "I've got yard time with my dad tonight." Sounds hard, not cute; passes with swagger. If a savvy friend says "like prison?", to a 14-year-old that's a feature, not embarrassment.

**Tagline pairing.** *"The Yard. Put in the work. Together."* Alt: *"Meet me in the Yard."*

**Risks.** (1) The prison-yard undertone that makes it cool to teens cuts the other way with parents and in the church-community wedge — this needs a gut-check with actual wedge dads before proceeding. (2) Generic dictionary word: search/ownability is hard, and existing brands sit on it (a restaurant chain, coworking spaces — REPORTED, UNVERIFIED). (3) "The ___" names are awkward in some app-store and social-handle contexts.

## 5. Recommendation

**Take Strongside and HomeTeam into verification together; let the trademark/domain sweep break the tie.** They are the two lanes: Strongside maximizes athletic register and teen credibility (the anti-teen-drift name); HomeTeam maximizes mission alignment and family identity (the north-star name). Both fully respect the wedge rule (father-son in marketing only). Run Pact through verification as the third — it's the only name whose failure would also teach us something about the product vocabulary.

Whichever survives cleanest wins. If both survive, the founder's call is: *whose shirt does the 14-year-old wear?* — and that argues Strongside.

## 6. Verification checklist (founder runs; none of this has been done)

Every availability claim above is UNVERIFIED. Before committing to any name:

1. **USPTO search** — uspto.gov trademark search (successor to TESS, at tmsearch.uspto.gov). Search exact name + phonetic near-matches in classes **9** (software), **41** (fitness/training services), **42** (SaaS), and **25** (apparel — merch matters for this brand). Check live *and* dead marks (a dead fitness mark, e.g. Pact, still tells you the history).
2. **Domain sweep** — registrar search (Namecheap/Porkbun/Instant Domain Search) for .com, .app, .co, plus get-/join-/the- variants. For parked .coms, check broker asking price before ruling out.
3. **App Store + Google Play** — search the exact name and near-spellings; note everything in the top 10, especially anything in Health & Fitness. An abandoned same-name fitness app is a blocker even if the TM is dead (App Store name uniqueness).
4. **Google + social sweep** — "[name] fitness", "[name] gym", "[name] app"; handle availability on Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, X; Urban Dictionary check for slang misfires.
5. **Common-law check** — state business registries and a plain search for gyms/coaches/CrossFit affiliates using the name (class 41 common-law rights don't need a registration to bite).
6. **In-house testers** — say each finalist out loud to the three resident kids (9, 10, 14) and 2–3 wedge-community dads. The 14-year-old's face is data. Also TTS-test the name in a coach voice line ("Welcome back to ___") — the AI coach will say this name thousands of times.
7. **Attorney knockout search** — before spending on logo/identity, a trademark attorney knockout search (~$500–1,500) on the single finalist.

## Addendum — founder-owned domains (2026-07-07)

The founder already owns, from earlier project work: **squadstrong.com**, **squadstrong.fit**, **kinforgefit.com**, **kinforge.fit**. Not committed to either name, but ownership removes the largest verification uncertainty (domain cost/availability) for these two — a real criterion every other candidate scores UNVERIFIED on.

**SquadStrong** — Strategy B (family-team language). Strengths: "squad" is genuinely teen-adjacent vocabulary, athletic register, no gender coding, speakable/spellable, and it dovetails with the already-adopted **Squad Consistency Bar** mechanic (church/men's-group squads) — the brand could *name* the community layer. Risks: "squad" as slang peaked late-2010s and may read slightly dated to a 2026 teen (test this — the 14-year-old's reaction is the data); the CamelCase compound has the same slightly-supplement-brand ring that held IronPact out of the top 5; likely crowded App Store space around "squad" fitness terms (UNVERIFIED). Rough score against §2 criteria: **~16/20**.

**Kinforge** — coined compound (Strategy E). Strengths: the most *ownable* name on the entire board (coined = clean trademark lane, total search ownability); the forge metaphor is deeply on-mission — strength forged through shared struggle is literally the brand story, and it hands the coach voice a gift ("forged, not given"); "kin" resonates with the family-values wedge communities. Risks: "kin" is precious/archaic to a teen ear — nobody 14 says kin; a mild fantasy-guild ring (test whether that's a bug or a feature with the boys); ideally wants kinforge.com, status UNVERIFIED (founder owns kinforgefit.com). Rough score: **~16/20**.

**Ruling for the verification sweep:** neither displaces Strongside/HomeTeam on pure brand quality, but ownership is worth real points at this stage — **add both to the dinner-table test alongside Strongside, HomeTeam, and Pact.** Five names, one evening, the 14-year-old's face ranks them. If Strongside/HomeTeam hit domain walls (parked, five-figure asks), SquadStrong and Kinforge become the pragmatic front-runners at zero acquisition cost. Either way, keep the unused domains — squadstrong.fit makes a fine beta-waitlist or league-landing URL even under another brand.

## Round 2 — founder-taste-directed (2026-07-07)

_Round 2 optimizes for the founder's taste profile (below), received after the round-1 slate and the owned-domains addendum. ### R2.1 The taste profile (why this round is different)

- **Compounds only.** Two-word/compound names; standalone single words read generic and hard to brand. Pact, Forge, Foundry, Crucible are out as brands — but in as *components*. (The Weekly Pact mechanic keeps its name regardless.)
- **The register exemplars are SquadStrong and Kinforge** (domains owned): {family/team word} × {strength/forge/metallurgy word}. From round 1 he likes Strongside, IronPact, and Forge/Foundry as components.
- **The HomeTeam lesson.** HomeTeam and Homefield read too generic to him — flat familiar-phrase compounds are dead on arrival; names need an edge. This retroactively re-ranks the round-1 slate (scores stand; the combined slate in R2.6 applies the filter).
- **The Crucible exception.** The one single word he loves — partly via Destiny/Destiny 2, where the Crucible is the competitive arena and Trials of Osiris its pinnacle event; this product also runs monthly Trials. Explored two ways: as compound components (R2.2) and as an in-product event name (ruling in R2.5).
- **The iron tension.** Drawn to iron names, but iron connotes barbells and v1 is bodyweight-pure. Not a lane-kill — assessed per name in R2.3's iron note.

### R2.5 The Crucible ruling — event name, not brand

**Compounding failed honestly** (see the kill table): Crucible's three Latinate syllables front-load every compound past the speakable bar, and each attempt sounds worse than the word alone. Crucible wants the definite article and a stage. That is an event name.

**The ruling: give "The Crucible" to the Challenge system — not to Trials, and not to the brand.**

- **Keep Trials untouched.** "Trials" is already perfect, owned vocabulary, and the flagship benchmark ritual (Trials 2.0's whole emotional arc is built on it). Renaming it would trade an owned word for a borrowed one — and it would *invert* the Destiny reference the founder loves: in Destiny, the Crucible is the standing arena and Trials is its pinnacle event (REPORTED). Renaming Trials "The Crucible" gets the homage backwards.
- **"Challenges" is the weakest word in the product vocabulary** — every app on earth has challenges. Renaming the earned max-effort event system "The Crucible" is a strict upgrade: distinctive, severe, earned. Copy mechanics work immediately: *"You've earned a Crucible."* / *"Crucible unlocked: The Family 500."* And the flagship Co-op format becomes the metaphor made literal — the whole family climbs into the crucible together; shared heat is what bonds metal. That is the brand story told by a feature name.
- **The pairing is the payoff.** Trials (sacred monthly benchmark) + The Crucible (earned max-effort arena) reads to a Destiny-playing 14-year-old as a wink from someone who gets it, and to a parent as the Marines (the Crucible — recruit training's 54-hour culminating test, REPORTED) and English class (Miller). Dual-register is exactly what the coach voice wants.
- **G2 sanity check:** G2 kills names whose *dominant* association is a video game (Kratos). Crucible's dominant association outside gaming remains metallurgy/Miller/Marines — the word predates and exceeds the game. Passes.

**Risk assessment (all REPORTED/UNVERIFIED):**

| Prior use | What it is | Risk for in-product feature use |
|---|---|---|
| Bungie's Crucible | Destiny/Destiny 2 PvP mode (2014–present); standalone registered-mark status UNVERIFIED | **Low** — a mode name inside their game, distant context. If "Enter the Crucible" ever becomes hero *marketing* copy, re-check first: the audiences overlap. |
| Amazon's Crucible | 2020 free-to-play shooter, cancelled the same year | **Low** — dead product; residual TM status UNVERIFIED. |
| Arthur Miller's *The Crucible* | 1953 play | **None** — no mark issue; the literary weight actually helps with parents. |

| The Crucible, Oakland | Industrial-arts school; plus plausible gyms/boxes named Crucible (UNVERIFIED) | **Low** for a feature name; would matter for a brand (class 41). |

Net: **low risk as an in-product system name; moderate-to-high as a brand** — which the compounds-only taste rule already forecloses. If adopted, `docs/design/trials-and-challenges.md` (§Challenges 2.0) picks up the rename at its next revision.
